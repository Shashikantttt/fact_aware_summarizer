Cross-Lingual Knowledge Transfer and Factuality Grounding in Low-Resource Indic Language Summarization

Dr. Rajeshwari Narayanaswamy, Department of Computer Science & Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Madras, Chennai, India. ORCID: 0000-0002-8419-7231
Siddharth Kulkarni, AI4Bharat Research Center, Indian Institute of Technology Madras, Chennai, India. ORCID: 0009-0004-1182-9903
Ananya Sengupta, Department of Computational Linguistics, Jadavpur University, Kolkata, India. ORCID: 0000-0003-4521-8890

Abstract—Natural language generation across the 22 constitutionally recognized Indian languages presents severe data-scarcity bottlenecks, morpho-syntactic richness, and complex agglutinative characteristics. While large pre-trained sequence-to-sequence neural architectures achieve high fluency in English, direct translation-based fine-tuning for low-resource Indic languages frequently produces hallucinated entities, misaligned numerical predicates, and gender inflection errors. In this paper, we propose a fact-grounded cross-lingual transfer framework combining parameter-efficient multilingual adapters with dense semantic sentence retrieval to preserve core propositional logic during document reduction. Our approach introduces a lightweight syntactic boundary regularizer that filters rhetorical discourse markers while anchoring abstractive claims to verified source propositions. Evaluated on the IndicBART and BharatBench benchmarks across Hindi, Tamil, and Bengali datasets, our method demonstrates a 4.8-point increase in factual faithfulness scores and a 2.3-point improvement in ROUGE-L over baseline translation models without requiring high-resource bilingual parallel corpora.

Keywords—Indic NLP, cross-lingual transfer, factual consistency, natural language inference, low-resource summarization.

I. INTRODUCTION
India represents one of the most linguistically diverse geographies globally, housing four major language families: Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Austroasiatic, and Tibeto-Burman. Despite having hundreds of millions of native speakers across Hindi, Marathi, Telugu, and Tamil, digital text corpora for automated summarization remain severely constrained compared to high-resource Indo-European languages [1]. Modern sequence-to-sequence transformers such as mT5 and IndicBART demonstrate impressive grammatical coherence, yet their autoregressive decoding layers exhibit noticeable vulnerability to factual hallucination [2]. In public administration, agricultural advisory, and vernacular healthcare dissemination, inaccurate summarization can distort critical dosage instructions or governmental entitlement deadlines [3].

Extractive baseline algorithms such as Term Frequency - Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) guarantee factual fidelity by isolating verbatim sentences directly from the source document [4]. However, extractive techniques often retain non-essential discourse particles and struggle to consolidate interconnected arguments scattered across multiple paragraphs. Conversely, purely abstractive architectures rephrase concepts into concise statements, but risk generating synthetic assertions that lack direct textual entailment in the original document.

II. CORPUS PREPARATION AND LINGUISTIC CHALLENGES
Our investigation focuses on multi-paragraph articles gathered from the IndicCorp v2 repository and vernacular press releases from the Press Information Bureau (PIB) Government of India. The primary linguistic challenges encountered include:
1. Agglutination and Morphological Complexity: Dravidian languages such as Tamil and Kannada concatenate postpositions, tense markers, and case suffixes into single orthographic words, requiring specialized subword tokenization to prevent out-of-vocabulary fragmentation.
2. Free Word Order: Unlike English with strict Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) typography, most Indic languages allow flexible Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) ordering, which complicates standard positional embedding projections in unidirectional decoders.
3. Named Entity Disambiguation: Transliterated proper nouns across Devanagari, Bengali, and Tamil scripts frequently introduce semantic drift, leading neural models to confuse administrative bodies with geographic landmarks [5].

III. CROSS-LINGUAL SUMMARIZATION & FACTUAL FIDELITY
To reconcile conciseness with factual integrity, we implement a two-stage verification pipeline. In the first phase, source documents undergo structured morphological cleaning, removing boilerplate navigation text, digital object identifiers, and publication citations. The primary informative content is then scored via TF-IDF salience with length normalization to establish a verified extractive baseline. In the second phase, abstractive candidates generated by fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence transformers are segmented into discrete propositional claims. Each claim is paired against top-ranked source premises retrieved via dense semantic embeddings. A Natural Language Inference (NLI) classifier evaluates whether each claim is entailed, contradicted, or ungrounded.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
We benchmarked our system against standard baseline models across 500 validated document-summary pairs drawn from Hindi, Bengali, and Tamil news corpora. On the Hindi evaluation split, our fact-grounded framework achieved a ROUGE-1 score of 42.6% and a ROUGE-L score of 38.4%, while reducing factual hallucination occurrences by 31.8% compared to unconstrained IndicBART. Furthermore, sentence retrieval latency remained under 120 milliseconds per document on commodity server hardware, demonstrating practical viability for real-time deployment in rural public service kiosks.
